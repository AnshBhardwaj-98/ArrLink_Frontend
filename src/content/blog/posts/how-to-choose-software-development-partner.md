**Choosing a software development partner** starts with evidence: relevant work, a clear understanding of your business problem, and a delivery plan you can evaluate. Before comparing proposals, define the first useful release, ask who will build it, and agree on how you will review progress. For US founders and businesses working with a remote team, communication hours and handover arrangements deserve the same attention as the technology stack.

This guide gives you questions to ask, a proposal comparison checklist, and a project brief you can send to potential partners. It is written for buyers planning a custom application, a SaaS product, or an improvement to an existing system.

## Start with the business problem, not a list of technologies

A useful brief explains who needs the software and what they need to accomplish. “Build a dashboard” leaves too much open. “Let our operations team see which customer orders need attention without combining three spreadsheets” gives a development team something concrete to investigate.

Before your first call, write down:

- **Users:** who will use the product, and who approves the work?
- **Current workflow:** what happens today, including manual steps?
- **First outcome:** what should someone be able to do after the first release?
- **Existing systems:** which applications, databases, or APIs must connect?
- **Constraints:** what budget range, deadline, or access limitation affects the work?
- **Acceptance:** how will you demonstrate that the release works?

For an order-management tool, an acceptance condition might be: an authorized user can import a sample order file, see invalid rows, correct them, and create orders without duplicates. That is more useful than an unexplained promise to deliver an “enterprise-ready platform.”

## Evaluate relevant projects and the team’s actual contribution

A portfolio is a starting point for a conversation. Ask what the company built, what another team supplied, and which parts resemble your own challenge.

Useful questions include:

1. What problem did the client bring to you?
2. Which components did your team design or implement?
3. What was the hardest integration or technical tradeoff?
4. Can you show a public product, a permitted walkthrough, or an anonymized example?
5. What changed after launch, and how was that handled?

## Ask why the proposed technology fits your business

A long list of programming languages tells you little about the architecture your project needs. Ask for a short explanation of the proposed stack and its tradeoffs.

| Area | Question to ask | What a useful answer includes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Web interface | Why this frontend framework? | User experience needs, existing code, and maintenance considerations |
| Backend | How will business rules and integrations work? | API boundaries, background tasks, and error handling |
| Data | How will data stay accurate and accessible? | A data model, permissions, migrations, and backup responsibilities |
| Deployment | How will we release and recover from problems? | Environments, monitoring, rollback steps, and account access |
| AI features | How will we know the feature is reliable enough? | Evaluation examples, failure behavior, human review, and usage limits |

You do not need to pick every tool before engaging a partner. You do need to understand why the choices suit your product and who will maintain them. Our [technology overview](/#technology) connects the tools ArrLink works with to the kinds of systems they support.

For a new subscription product, discuss billing, account permissions, and tenant separation early. These are useful topics for a [SaaS development](/services/saas-development) conversation, even when the initial feature set is small.

## Compare proposals against the same scope

Two quotes are not directly comparable if one includes design, testing, deployment, and handover while the other covers implementation alone.

Ask each shortlisted partner to respond to the same brief. Use the following checklist to record what is included, excluded, or still uncertain.

| Proposal item | Evidence to request |
| --- | --- |
| Deliverables | Named workflows or features, with acceptance conditions |
| Assumptions | Dependencies on your team, third parties, and existing systems |
| Delivery team | Roles, responsibilities, and the person handling day-to-day questions |
| Schedule | Milestones, review dates, and dependencies that could change them |
| Commercial scope | Currency, payment milestones, and treatment of scope changes |
| Running costs | Hosting, API usage, software subscriptions, and ongoing support |
| Quality checks | What will be tested, by whom, and before which release |
| Handover | Repository access, documentation, deployment steps, and account transfer |

For a US buyer, requesting the proposal in USD can make comparison easier. Also ask who pays for third-party services and whether those charges sit outside the development quote.

Treat an estimate as a set of assumptions to review. If an integration is undocumented, a responsible proposal should identify that uncertainty rather than hide it behind a precise delivery date.

## Make remote communication specific

“Available in your time zone” is too vague to plan around. Agree on the actual meeting window, how decisions are recorded, and what happens when a question blocks progress.

A practical communication plan can include:

- A named contact on each side.
- An agreed overlap window, expressed in time zones rather than ambiguous local times.
- Written progress updates showing completed work, next steps, and blockers.
- Demonstrations tied to milestones, using a working environment where possible.
- A shared place for decisions and change requests.
- A separate escalation process for production incidents if support is included.

Check how daylight saving changes affect recurring meetings. Do not assume a development engagement includes round-the-clock support; agree on support coverage separately.

The important question is whether the arrangement fits your team’s working day and gives both sides a reliable way to resolve decisions.

## Discuss security and handover before development starts

Ask how the team controls access, reviews changes, protects credentials, and handles security issues. Request examples appropriate to the sensitivity of your product rather than accepting a generic “secure by default” statement.

[NIST’s Secure Software Development Framework](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/sp/800/218/final) provides a vocabulary buyers and suppliers can use when discussing secure development. Its practices cover organizational preparation, protecting software, producing secure software, and responding to vulnerabilities. Use it as a discussion reference; mentioning the framework is not evidence that a vendor has implemented its practices.

For handover, establish who will administer the source repository, cloud accounts, domain, and third-party integrations. Ask what documentation a new engineer would receive and how a release could be deployed after the engagement ends.

Ownership and license terms should be explicit in the agreement. Do not infer them from access to a repository or from the phrase “custom software.”

## Start with a focused milestone when important questions remain

A smaller first engagement can give both teams a concrete way to evaluate the working relationship. Choose a milestone that resolves a meaningful uncertainty.

Examples include:

- Testing whether a required API supports the workflow you need.
- Designing and validating the main user journey before implementing it.
- Delivering one complete internal workflow with realistic sample data.
- Reviewing an existing application and producing a prioritized improvement plan.

Define the output and review criteria before work starts. A prototype should be described as a prototype, with its limitations recorded. A production feature needs its own testing and release requirements.

## A project brief you can send today

Copy the following outline into your first inquiry. Short, concrete answers are enough to start a useful conversation.

> **Business problem:** What is difficult or slow today?
>
> **Users:** Who needs the software, and who will approve it?
>
> **First release:** Which three capabilities matter most?
>
> **Existing systems:** What needs to connect or be reused?
>
> **Success check:** What should we demonstrate at the first milestone?
>
> **Constraints:** What budget range, deadline, or data-access limitation should we discuss?
>
> **Communication:** What is your time zone and preferred meeting window?

## Frequently asked questions

### What should I look for in a software development partner?

Look for relevant work, clear explanations of technical decisions, and a proposal with testable deliverables. Ask who will do the work and how progress, changes, and handover will be managed. Evaluate the answers against your project’s needs rather than portfolio size alone.

### Can a small development company be a good fit?

Yes, if the team has the relevant skills and enough capacity for the agreed scope. Ask about current availability, responsibilities, and coverage when a key person is unavailable. A focused first milestone can help you assess the working relationship before committing to a larger scope.

### How can a US business work effectively with an overseas development team?

Agree on meeting hours, written updates, decision ownership, and milestone demonstrations before kickoff. Specify time zones and review how seasonal clock changes affect the schedule. Discuss production support separately from everyday project communication.

### Should I choose the lowest development quote?

Compare quotes against the same deliverables and assumptions first. Check whether design, testing, deployment, documentation, third-party costs, and support are included. A lower price may reflect a smaller scope; ask the team to explain the difference.

### Do I need to know my technology stack before contacting a partner?

No. Bring your business workflow, user needs, existing systems, and constraints. A prospective partner should explain a suitable approach and its tradeoffs, including whether your existing stack can support the next release.
